In 2027, bank holidays and stock market holidays are not the same thing. Banks, the Federal Reserve, the stock market and the bond market each run on slightly different calendars, and the gaps between them matter if you are timing a payment, a trade, or a wire transfer.

This guide sorts out who closes when, where the schedules diverge, and how to avoid getting caught by a surprise closure.

## Banks and the Federal Reserve

Most U.S. banks follow the Federal Reserve's holiday schedule, which observes all 11 federal holidays. On those days, Fedwire and the automated clearing house that moves most electronic payments do not process transfers, so anything you send is queued to the next business day.

There is one important quirk. When a holiday falls on a Saturday, the Federal Reserve does not move the day off to the Friday before. Federal employees may get that Friday, but banks generally stay open. In 2027 this affects Juneteenth and Christmas, both of which fall on a Saturday. Juneteenth is observed by the Fed on the actual Saturday, so the preceding Friday, June 18, is a normal banking day.

The practical takeaway: do not assume a bank is closed just because your office is. Check the Federal Reserve date, not the federal observed date.

## The stock market

The New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq publish their own holiday calendar, and it is not the same as the bank calendar in two directions.

First, the stock market stays open on two federal holidays: Columbus Day and Veterans Day. Banks close; the exchanges trade normally.

Second, the stock market closes on one day that is not a federal holiday at all: Good Friday, which in 2027 falls on March 26. Equity markets are shut even though banks and government offices are open.

The exchanges also run shortened sessions around some holidays, typically closing early the day before Independence Day, Thanksgiving, and Christmas when those fall on a weekday.

## The bond market

Fixed-income markets follow the schedule recommended by SIFMA, the industry association, and it splits the difference between the other two. The bond market observes the bank holidays, but it also recommends closures or early closes on days the stock market trades, including Columbus Day and Veterans Day, and recommends early closes ahead of several holidays.

If you trade or settle bonds, treasuries, or money-market instruments, the SIFMA calendar is the one to watch, because it will not line up with either your bank or the equity market on every date.

## Where the 2027 calendars disagree

A few dates are worth flagging because at least one market behaves differently from the others:

- **Good Friday, March 26:** stock market closed, banks open.
- **Columbus Day, October 11:** banks closed, stock market open, bond market closed.
- **Veterans Day, November 11:** banks closed, stock market open, bond market closed.
- **Friday, June 18 (day before Saturday Juneteenth):** banks open, markets open; only some employers give the day.

## Why the calendars don't line up

The three schedules exist for different reasons, which is why they never fully agree. The Federal Reserve calendar is built around federal employees and the payment system, so it tracks the official federal holidays and the observed-day rules that come with them. The stock exchanges set their own trading calendar around market activity and tradition, which is why they keep trading on Columbus Day and Veterans Day, two low-volume days, but close for Good Friday, a day of thin global activity. The bond market, coordinated through SIFMA's recommendations, leans toward giving fixed-income desks time off and so closes on more days than the stock market.

For most people the only practical consequence is settlement timing. A stock trade executed on Columbus Day is perfectly valid, but the cash leg still has to clear through the banking system, which is closed, so the money moves on the next banking day. The reverse happens on Good Friday: banks are open and can move money, but there is no equity market to trade in.

If you deal with overseas counterparties, add one more layer: foreign markets follow their own national holidays, so a U.S. trading day can still stall if the other side's bank or exchange is shut.

## How to plan around them

For payments, assume that anything routed through a bank or the ACH network will not move on a Federal Reserve holiday, and build in an extra business day before a long weekend. A payroll run or a bill due on a Tuesday after a Monday holiday effectively loses Monday as a processing day.

For trades, remember that a closed bank does not mean a closed market, and vice versa. The two days most likely to trip people up are Columbus Day and Veterans Day, when the market is open but money cannot settle through banks, and Good Friday, when the reverse is true.

When in doubt, confirm the specific institution's calendar. Your own bank, broker, and employer each publish their observed dates, and those are the ones that actually govern your money.
