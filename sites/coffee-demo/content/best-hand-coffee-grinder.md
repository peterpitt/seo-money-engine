## What actually matters when choosing

### Burr material and shape

Almost every hand grinder uses conical burrs. Stainless steel burrs are the default for good reason: they stay sharp, resist chipping, and are easy to clean.

Ceramic burrs never rust, but they chip more easily and struggle with very light roasts. If the budget allows, go steel.

### Bearings and shaft stability

A wobbly shaft changes the gap between the burrs on every turn. That means a mix of dust and boulders in the same dose.

Dual bearings keep the shaft centered, which is the single biggest difference between a cheap grinder and a great one. Hold the handle and try to wiggle it before you buy.

### Click settings

Stepped settings let you return to the exact same grind after switching beans. That makes dialing in faster, especially for beginners.

Finer steps help with pour-over and espresso. If you only brew French press, you do not need dozens of tiny clicks.

### Capacity and weight

A single cup of pour-over uses about 15 to 20 grams of coffee. If you often brew for two, look for a hopper that holds at least 30 grams.

For travel, weight matters more than anything. Anything under about a pound fits easily in a backpack.

### Parts and support

Burrs dull over time, and gaskets wear out. Check that replacement parts are sold in your country before you commit to a brand.

## Hand grinder vs electric: which should you buy?

A hand grinder makes sense if you brew one or two cups at a time. It is quiet, needs no outlet, and puts more of your budget into the burrs instead of a motor.

At the same price, a hand grinder usually beats an electric one on grind quality. Electric grinders at entry prices often cut corners on burr alignment.

Electric becomes the better choice once you brew three or more cups at once, or when grinding by hand starts to feel like a chore. Speed is worth paying for if it means you actually brew every morning.

## Common mistakes when buying

The most common mistake is buying for espresso when you only make pour-over. Espresso-capable grinders cost more, and the extra precision goes unused.

Another trap is judging by the number of clicks. A grinder with many settings but a loose shaft will still produce uneven grounds.

Finally, people forget about cleaning. If the burrs are hard to remove, you will clean them less often, and stale oils will slowly creep into every cup.

## How to test a grinder at home

You do not need lab gear to judge grind quality. Grind one dose at your usual setting and spread it on a sheet of white paper.

Look for consistency. A good grinder gives particles of similar size with little powder. A weak one leaves a cloud of dust next to visible chunks.

Then brew the same beans twice, one click apart. If the grinder is consistent, you should taste a clear, predictable change rather than a random swing between sour and bitter.

## Getting the most out of your grinder

Season a new grinder by running a cheap bag of beans through it. This smooths out machining marks so flavor stays consistent later.

Tap the body after each use to clear old grounds. Skip the water and use a brush or air blower instead, so the burrs never rust.

Write down your settings. Every bean and brew method has a sweet spot, and notes let you repeat a great cup instead of guessing.

## Buying checklist

Before you order, run through these questions. If every answer is yes, you are ready to buy:

- Are the burrs stainless steel, and does the shaft feel solid?
- Can you return to the same setting easily?
- Does the hopper fit the number of cups you brew at once?
- Are spare burrs and parts available where you live?
- Does the price fit your budget tier without paying for features you will never use?
